How to read expected goals (xG) before you trust a prediction

By GoalPre analysis desk · 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

Expected goals is the single most useful number in modern football analysis — and also the one most often quoted without being understood. Here is how we read it, and the traps we watch for.

Contents

  2. Why one number hides two different stories

1. What xG actually measures

Expected goals (xG) puts a value between 0 and 1 on every shot, based on how often shots from that position and situation have historically been scored. A tap-in from six yards might be worth 0.7; a hopeful effort from 30 yards might be worth 0.03. Add up every shot a team takes in a match and you get its xG for that game — a measure of the quality of chances it created, not the goals it happened to score.

That distinction is the whole point. Goals are rare and noisy; a team can play well and lose 1-0, or play badly and win 3-0 off three deflections. xG strips away some of that luck and asks a cleaner question: how good were the chances? Over a run of games, the team creating 1.8 xG a match and conceding 0.9 is almost always the better side, whatever the odd scoreline says.

2. Why one number hides two different stories

The mistake we see most often is quoting a team’s xG as if it were a single verdict. It is really two numbers that must be read together:

  • xG for — the quality of chances a team creates. High is good.
  • xG against — the quality of chances it lets the opponent have. Low is good.

A side averaging 2.1 xG for looks fearsome until you notice it also concedes 1.9 xG against — that is a thrill-a-minute team whose games fly over 2.5 goals, not a reliable winner. Another side on 1.3 for and 0.7 against scores less but controls games and wins tight ones. Same league, opposite betting profiles. We never look at one half of the pair without the other.

Quick rule: for a result lean, watch the gap between xG for and xG against. For an Over/Under lean, watch the combined total of both teams’ xG for — two open 1.8-xG attacks point to goals; two cagey 0.9-xG sides point under.

3. The sample-size trap

xG needs games to settle down. Over three matches a single freak result drags the average around; over fifteen it starts to describe the team honestly. Early in a season, or right after a managerial change, we treat xG as a hint rather than a fact and lean more on longer-term form. When someone shows you a glittering xG number built on four games, be sceptical — it is as likely to be variance as a trend.

We also separate home and away xG. Many teams are a different animal on the road — more conservative, fewer and lower-quality chances — so a blended figure can flatter an away trip. A team’s away xG is the honest guide to how it will play away.

4. How we use it in a prediction

In our workflow xG is an input, never the whole answer. It feeds the model alongside shot rates, form and head-to-head, and then a human reads it against the things xG cannot see: injuries, fixture congestion, whether a side is already safe or desperate for points. xG tells you how well a team has been playing; it does not know that the first-choice striker is out or that the game is a dead rubber.

So when you see one of our fixtures flagged as a high-confidence pick, the quality gap in xG is usually part of the reason — but only part.
